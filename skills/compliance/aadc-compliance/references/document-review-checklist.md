# Document review checklist

Use this checklist for child DPIAs and risk reviews, privacy information written for children and parents, terms of service and community standards, parental-control disclosures, UX designs and prototypes, age-assurance policies, and connected-toy packaging and set-up material.

Check two things for each document: that it contains what the applicable code expects, and that it matches what the product actually does. A DPIA that describes defaults the code does not ship, or omits a feature children use, is a finding. When both a document and code are in scope, cross-check them against the step 2 inventory.

## Child DPIA and risk review (ICO 1-2; Cal. §1798.99.31(a)(1)-(4) where in force; Maryland Kids Code; DSA 28 guidelines)

- [ ] Describes each feature children use and the data it processes
- [ ] Identifies the age ranges likely to use the service, with the evidence
- [ ] Assesses best interests explicitly, across the UN CRC rights the ICO and the Commission point to: safety, health and wellbeing, privacy, development, play, freedom from commercial exploitation
- [ ] Covers the risks the codes name: harmful content, harmful contacts and conduct, profiling, persuasive design, commercial exploitation, location exposure
- [ ] Records the mitigation for each risk and the residual risk, and the age-assurance level chosen for the risk
- [ ] Consulted children or parents, or explains why not
- [ ] Is dated, owned, and has a review trigger such as a new feature or default change
- [ ] Is available to the regulator on request where a statute requires it (California and Maryland laws set delivery timeframes; check live)

## Privacy information for children (ICO 4; Cal. (a)(7))

- [ ] Versions suited to the age ranges in the DPIA: plain language, short, visual where useful
- [ ] Bite-sized explanations at the point a feature collects data or changes a setting
- [ ] Separate information for parents where parents are involved
- [ ] Explains each default and what changing it means
- [ ] Explains profiling, location and parental monitoring when present

## Terms, policies and community standards (ICO 6; Cal. (a)(9); DSA Art. 14 and 28 guidelines)

- [ ] Written so the youngest likely users can understand the rules that apply to them
- [ ] Enforcement matches the published standards; a moderation policy that differs from the terms is a finding
- [ ] Reporting and appeal routes are described and match the product

## Parental controls (ICO 11; Cal. (a)(8))

- [ ] Explains to the child what a parent can see, in age-appropriate words
- [ ] Explains to the parent the child's right to privacy and the signal shown to the child
- [ ] Presents parental tools as a complement to service-level protections

## UX designs and prototypes (ICO 7, 10, 12, 13; DSA 28 guidelines)

- [ ] Every settings screen shows the child default at the high-privacy value
- [ ] Choice screens give the high-privacy option equal or greater prominence
- [ ] No streaks, countdowns, loss framing or social pressure designed to extend use or collect data
- [ ] Location, contact and visibility features show their state clearly

## Age-assurance policy (ICO 3; Cal. (a)(5); DSA 28 guidelines)

- [ ] Ties the method to the risk assessment
- [ ] States accuracy evidence for the method or vendor
- [ ] Limits the data's use and retention to age assurance
- [ ] Provides a route to challenge a wrong result

## Connected toys and devices (ICO 14)

- [ ] Packaging or pre-purchase information explains data collection, cloud use and who receives the data
- [ ] Set-up explains recording, listening and transmission before activation
- [ ] Explains how to delete data and reset the device for a new owner

## National guidance (EU)

For Ireland-led processing, test the documents against the DPC Fundamentals (for example, a floor of protection for all users rather than age-gating alone, and no reliance on legitimate interests where processing interferes with a child's best interests). For Dutch-facing services, the Code voor Kinderrechten principles are a voluntary benchmark; cite them as good practice, not as obligations.
